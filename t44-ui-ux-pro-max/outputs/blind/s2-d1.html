The kiln sits 40 steps from the tide line. Humidity is part of the recipe, not a problem to solve.

One kick wheel, one wood kiln, no assembly line.

I'm the only person who touches a piece from clay to glaze. The stoneware is dug from a coastal seam an hour north, wedged by hand, and thrown slow enough that the wheel marks stay visible: the record of a hand having been there.

Firing happens six times a year in a single-chamber anagama kiln that takes 30 hours to reach 1,285°C. Ash from the driftwood fuel lands unevenly across the shelf, which is why no two pieces from the same firing ever glaze the same.

How it's made

Four stages, thirty-odd hours, no shortcuts.

Dig & Wedge

Coastal stoneware clay, hand-wedged to knock out air pockets before it ever touches the wheel.

Throw

Centered and pulled on a kick wheel, slower than electric, which is why the walls stay a touch uneven.

Bisque & Glaze

First firing hardens the form; glaze is mixed from local ash and dipped by hand, once.

Wood-fire

30 hours in the anagama kiln at 1,285°C. The fire decides the final color, not the recipe.
